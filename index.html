Why sixty points, and not any improvement at all? Take one student on two different Saturdays with no studying in between: their score can move about sixty points by itself. A smaller rise would not show that the teaching did anything, so we do not count it.

The diagnostic

Every tutoring company runs a diagnostic. Almost all of them stop at the number.

A number can’t see that your child knew the material and simply ran out of clock. It can’t see that four of those right answers were lucky guesses. It can’t see that the same student, on a different Saturday, scores forty points higher for no reason at all. Two students can hand in the same 1180 and need opposite things. All of that is measurable — it just takes more work than printing a score and naming a weak topic. Your report measures it: every question timed, every finding weighed against how much of it could be luck. So when it does point at something, that thing is real.

Our statistical methods:

Four questions can’t give you a percentage.

Your child answers four questions on one topic and gets three wrong. Most reports print 25% and move on — but four questions is barely better than a coin toss, and the true number could be anywhere from 5% to 70%. That range has a name, a Wilson interval, and it is printed beside every area we name: narrow means we know, wide means we do not. Look at eight topics through samples that small and one will always look alarming by chance alone, so each is first pulled back toward your child’s own average, hardest where the evidence is thinnest. It has to still look bad afterwards before we will name it — which is why an honest report sometimes names nothing.

A right answer can still be a problem.

Mistakes are not all the same thing. Some questions your child genuinely did not know. Some they got right — but it took three times longer than it should have, and that time came straight out of the questions at the end. Some they never really reached. Each one needs a different week of work, so the report counts them separately and shows you every question in each pile.

We start where the points are.

The topic with the worst percentage is usually the smallest corner of the test. Algebra is about twice the size of geometry on every SAT, so the same five mistakes are worth twice as much there — and algebra can be taught from scratch in a way circle theorems cannot. So we rank by what the points are worth and how teachable they are. A hard question never counts as easy just because its topic usually is.

A gain has to beat a good morning.

The same student, on two different days, having learned nothing in between, can score sixty points apart. So under sixty points your portal says steady — and still shows you the number it refused to call progress. Over sixty, the gain is real. That is the one number everybody else rounds in their own favor.

You get all of it from a free diagnostic — one timed sitting — and the report is yours whether or not we ever work together. If we do, those same charts stay open in your portal as the work happens.
