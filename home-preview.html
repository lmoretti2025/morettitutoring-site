How I tutor

Most SAT tutors work through a fixed plan. I don’t.

The usual way is predictable: meet each week, cover the next topic, move down the list. For a student already scoring well, most of those hours go to material they already know.

My sessions follow your student. Every wrong answer tells me something about how they were thinking, not just what they got wrong, and the next question I give them is chosen to close exactly that gap. After years of teaching one-on-one, I know what each mistake means.

The diagnostic, and every test after it

Plenty of tutors say they adapt like an algorithm. I built one.

Every practice test records how each question was answered, not just whether it was right. Strong students rarely lose points to what they don’t know. They lose them here:

  • The clock. One stubborn question, and the next three get rushed.
  • Second guesses. Right answers changed to wrong ones at review.
  • Guesses. Answers given faster than the question can be read.
  • The hardest questions. Topics that require specific, focused, and repeatable approaches.

The course

The SAT asks the same questions in a hundred disguises.

The test returns to the same core concepts again and again. So instead of skimming a long list of topics, the course takes each one the SAT tests most and develops it fully, until every version of it looks familiar. Linear equations alone gets four lessons.

Every lesson is a video, a set of notes and a check at the end, built around real SAT questions.

The course map
Lesson 2.1 · checkSample question

A line in the xy-plane has a slope of 3 and passes through the point (2, 7). What is the y-coordinate of the line’s y-intercept?

Subtracted in the wrong order. 3(2) − 7 = −1. The intercept is 7 − 3(2).

Correct. Put the point into y = 3x + b: 7 = 3(2) + b, so b = 1.

Used the point’s y-value. 7 is the line’s height at x = 2. The y-intercept is its height at x = 0.

Went the wrong way. Moving from x = 2 back to x = 0 lowers y by 3 × 2 = 6, so 7 − 6, not 7 + 6.

Pick an answer. The × crosses a choice out.

Inside every lesson

Every wrong answer has a name.

Try the question. Whatever you pick, you’ll see where each wrong choice comes from. That is how the whole course is taught.

  • Recognize the question type. Every lesson starts from how the SAT actually words it, so your student knows it the moment it appears.

Questions

What parents usually ask.

What does it cost?
Sessions are billed by the hour, individually or from a prepaid pack, and how many your student needs depends on where they start and where they are aiming. So you see the plan and the total in writing before anything is scheduled. The diagnostic is free, and the report is yours either way. The payment terms are in the terms.
Why one tutor instead of a company?
Every session is with me. The person who reads your student’s data is the person who teaches them, every week. There are no handoffs to junior tutors and no rotating staff.
Online or in person?
Online, or in person at the Cranford Public Library. Same sessions, same materials, same portal, and many families mix the two.
When should we start?
Ideally three to four months before the test. Juniors usually start once PSAT scores come back, aiming for a March or May SAT. After the diagnostic I’ll give you a realistic timeline for your student.
Does the PSAT matter for National Merit?
For strong sophomores and juniors, yes. National Merit eligibility comes from the junior-year PSAT, and New Jersey’s cutoff is one of the highest in the country, so a student near it should prepare for the October PSAT as seriously as for the SAT.
How will I know it’s working?
You get an update after every session: what we worked on, how the practice went, and any new score. Every practice test lands in the portal with the same full report as the diagnostic.
