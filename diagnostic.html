Why a range

The same student, on two Saturdays, with no studying in between, moves about sixty points on their own. A single number pretends that variation does not exist.

So the report prints 1210, most likely 1170–1250. The width of that band is the honest answer to “what would they get next week?” — and it is why the guarantee is written against sixty points rather than any improvement at all.

Why this order

The topic with the worst percentage is usually the smallest corner of the test. Algebra is about twice the size of geometry on every SAT, so the same five mistakes are worth roughly twice as much there — and algebra can be taught from scratch in a way circle theorems cannot.

So skills are ranked by what the points are worth and how teachable they are, not by which percentage looks worst. That ranking is the session plan.

Why it matters

Every one of those twenty-four looks identical on a score report: a wrong answer. They need three completely different weeks of work, and a program that treats them the same will spend most of its time on the wrong one.

This student would be handed more content by most tutors. Thirteen of their twenty-four misses are clock problems, not knowledge problems. More content would have made it worse.

Four questions can’t give you a percentage

Look at row four. Two of four is 50% — and four questions is barely better than a coin toss. The true figure could sit anywhere from roughly 15% to 85%. That range has a name, a Wilson interval, and it is printed beside every area the report names: narrow means we know, wide means we do not.

Look at eight topics through samples that small and one will always look alarming by chance alone. So each is first pulled back toward the student’s own average, hardest where the evidence is thinnest. It has to still look bad afterwards before we will name it — which is why an honest report sometimes names nothing at all.

What this tells us

The plain-English page
  • Sample Student answered 74 of 98 correctly (76%), for a composite of 1210 with a likely range of 1170–1250.
  • Already strong: Central Ideas and Details, Inferences, Advanced Math. Keep these sharp with light review — there is nothing to win here.
  • Boundaries: 2 of 8 correct, about 8% of the test. Worth roughly 80 points, and it is a rules-based skill that can be taught in two sessions. This is week one.
  • Pacing: 9 questions were answered correctly but at three times the necessary pace, and 4 were never reached. Those two facts are the same fact.
  • Under-confidence: 6 questions were answered fast and wrong, and 5 flagged as uncertain were right. This student knows more than they are scoring.
  • Session time is weighted toward the ranked list above, with re-checks in weeks 6 and 11 to confirm each area is genuinely closed rather than assumed closed.
The point

Two students can hand in the same 1210 and need opposite things. One needs content, one needs a stopwatch, one needs to stop second-guessing. All of that is measurable — it just takes more work than printing a score and naming a weak topic.
